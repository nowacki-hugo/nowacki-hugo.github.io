Exoplanet characterisation

The rush for exoplanets started more than 30 years ago with the discovery of the 51 Peg b exoplanet (meaning a planet outside of our solar system), a historic result by Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz crowned with half of the 2019 Nobel prize in Physics. The other half rewarding James Peebles for his discovery of the cosmic microwave background. Since then, more than six thousands exoplanets have been discovered, as of 2026. Not only do we detect them, but the exoplanet community found ways to characterise them in order to adress a question that has become very pressing in the community : "Are we alone ?".

My work in this field is very limited and rich at the same time : based on my expertise in optical interferometry, I perform the most basic and ancient study this technique has allowed : stellar diameter measurements. But how is measuring the diameter of stars a way to characterize exoplanets ? Well, it is a tortuous path we are about to follow, and beware to whoever is not a professional astronomer : this might be painful, and you may skip the next section.

From the stellar diameter to the planet interior

The two most basic properties of planet interior models are the radius and the mass of the planet. In order to obtain those, the two main techniques are radial velocities and planetary transits. While the study of the radial velocity of the star provides an indication on the mass of the planet affecting the bulk motion of the star measured through the Doppler shift of spectral lines (ref), the detailed study of exoplanets transits provides great constraints on the planet's radius relative to the radius of the star, but also on the stellar density. The combination of both observations is necessary to constrain the planet interior. Unfortunately, observing a planet in transit requires that the orbit of the planet is aligned with the star and the line of sight from Earth, which is quite rare. Nevertheless, more than a thousand planets have been detected with both methods as of 2026, which is already a decent sample to explore the diversity of Physics at stake in planets formation, composition and evolution.

For those stars that are observed by both methods, a fundamental parameter is paramount in order to express properties of the planet on their own : the stellar radius \(R_*\). From geometric considerations, it can be shown that during the transit, the stellar photometry reaches a depth \($\Delta$F = { R_p \over R_* }\)
