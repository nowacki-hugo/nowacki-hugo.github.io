Are we alone in the Universe ?

The rush for exoplanets started more than 30 years ago with the discovery of the 51 Peg b exoplanet (meaning a planet outside of our solar system), a historic result by Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz crowned with half of the 2019 Nobel prize in Physics. The other half rewarding James Peebles for his discovery of the cosmic microwave background. Since then, more than six thousands exoplanets have been discovered, as of 2026. Not only do we detect them, but the exoplanet community found ways to characterise them in order to find out what they're made of and ultimately address a question that has become very pressing in the community :

Are we alone in the Universe ?

My work in this field is very limited and rich at the same time : based on my expertise in optical interferometry, I perform the most basic and ancient study this technique has allowed : stellar diameter measurements. But how is measuring the diameter of stars a way to characterise exoplanets ? Well, it is a tortuous path we are about to follow, and to whoever is not a professional astronomer : beware, this might be painful and you may skip the next section.

From the stellar radius to the planet interior composition

The two most basic properties of planet interior models are the radius and the mass of the planet \(R_p\) and \(M_p\). They are often displayed in a mass-radius diagram on which different compositions are shown, like in the famously overloaded figure from Zeng et al. (2019). In order to obtain those parameters, the two main techniques are radial velocities and planetary transits. While the study of the radial velocity of the star provides an indication on the mass of the planet affecting the bulk motion of the star measured through the Doppler shift of spectral lines (Mayor & Queloz, 1995), the detailed study of exoplanets transits provides great constraints on the planet's radius relative to the radius of the star, but also on the stellar density (Seager & Mallén-Ornelas, 2003). The combination of both observations is necessary to constrain the planet interior. Unfortunately, observing a planet in transit requires that the orbit of the planet is aligned with the star and the line of sight from Earth, which is quite rare. Nevertheless, more than a thousand planets have been detected with both methods as of 2026, which is a decent sample to explore the diversity of Physics at stake in planets formation, composition and evolution.

Even though it is overloaded, this figure illustrates the richness of the Physics that can be adressed here.

For those stars that are observed by both methods, the stellar radius \(R_*\) is paramount in order to obtain the properties of the planet. From geometric considerations, it can be shown that during the transit, the stellar photometry reaches a depth \(\sqrt{\Delta F} = \frac{R_p}{R_*}\), and therefore, knowing the stellar radius is required to know the radius of the planet. We can also notice that beyond the importance of the stellar radius, the precision of the planet's radius is also driven by our precision in photometry (hence the importance of space missions such as TESS or Plato). From Kepler's third law and Newton's second principle, it can be showed that the planet's mass is connected to the amplitude of radial velocity observed \(K\), the inclination, period and eccentricity of the orbit (\(i\), \(P\) and \(e\), respectively) and the stellar mass \(M_*\) through : \( M_p \propto \frac{K\sqrt{1-e^2}}{\sin{i}} \left(P M_*^2\right)^{1/3} \). Note that in this expression, \(K\), \(P\) and the eccentricity of the orbit come from the radial velocity of the star while the inclination of the orbit comes from the transit light curve. Finally, the stellar mass can be obtained from the combination of the stellar density \(\rho_*\), also obtained through the transit light curve and... the radius of the star ! \( \left(M_* = \frac{4\pi}{3} \rho_* R_*^3 \right) \)

Measuring the stellar radius

We have seen that knowing both the planet's mass and radius require to know the stellar radius. Unfortunately this is not what we measure, as no one has ever been close to a star and measured it with a ruler (I strongly advise not to try this...). What can be measured though, is the angular diameter of a star \(\theta\), which is the angle under which a star is perceived in the sky. This angle is directly linked to the stellar radius through the geometric relation \(R_* = \theta d\), where \(d\) is the distance of the star to the Earth... Yet another quantity, which is nowadays very well known for more than 2 billion (!) stars thanks to the Gaia space mission. This angle however is typically smaller than a millionth of degree. For reference, this is how small you would see a rather tall human (1.82 m) walking at the surface of the Moon, from Earth ! Measuring such a small angle requires an extremely high angular resolution technique, and interferometry is the best one to this date.
